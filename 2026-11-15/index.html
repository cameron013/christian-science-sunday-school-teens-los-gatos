00|Lesson Summary

The main message was “Throwing away the old clothes represents coming full circle out of poverty, blindness, and a very limited perspective on life.”

We focused on the Bible story of Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46-52). He sat by the road in poverty and blindness, and in a very limited view of life. When Jesus called, “he, casting away his garment, rose, and came to Jesus” (Mark 10:50). He did not patch the cloak. He left it behind.

We also used the light spectrum analogy to add color to this story.

We used the illustration of the fish asking “What's water?” from David Foster Wallace's 2005 Kenyon College commencement speech, “This Is Water.” The line we sat with is:

We explored the lesson through three questions:

  1. What are we throwing off? Bartimaeus did not patch his cloak. He threw it off. Old labels, fears, and grudges were never part of who we are, so we do not have to hug our tatters close. “Let us disrobe error. Then, when the winds of God blow, we shall not hug our tatters close about us.” (Science and Health 201)
  2. What are we putting on? We are never left standing there with nothing. When the old garment goes, we are “clothed upon” with what God already gave us: light, life, and gladness. “For we that are in this tabernacle do groan, being burdened: not for that we would be unclothed, but clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life.” (II Corinthians 5:4)
  3. Why Father Mother God? God is the source of every good quality, strength and tenderness, wisdom and love, so both “Father” and “Mother” are needed to say it. “In divine Science, we have not as much authority for considering God masculine, as we have for considering Him feminine, for Love imparts the clearest idea of Deity.” (Science and Health 517:8)

Teaching notes

An outline of the three questions, the illustration, and the case study, with the full citations. Written after class on November 15, 2026.

Three questions

1. What are we throwing off? Bartimaeus did not patch his cloak. He threw it off. Old labels, fears, and grudges were never part of who we are, so we do not have to hug our tatters close.

  • “And he, casting away his garment, rose, and came to Jesus.” (Mark 10:50)
  • “Let us disrobe error. Then, when the winds of God blow, we shall not hug our tatters close about us.” (Science and Health 201)
  • “By putting ‘off the old man with his deeds,’ mortals ‘put on immortality.’” (Science and Health 262:7)

2. What are we putting on? We are never left standing there with nothing. When the old garment goes, we are “clothed upon” with what God already gave us: light, life, and gladness.

  • “. . . this mortal must put on immortality.” (I Corinthians 15:53, Golden Text)
  • “For we that are in this tabernacle do groan, being burdened: not for that we would be unclothed, but clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life.” (II Corinthians 5:4)
  • “The night is far spent, the day is at hand: let us therefore cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armour of light.” (Romans 13:12)

3. Why Father Mother God? God is the source of every good quality, strength and tenderness, wisdom and love, so both “Father” and “Mother” are needed to say it.

  • “The ideal man corresponds to creation, to intelligence, and to Truth. The ideal woman corresponds to Life and to Love. In divine Science, we have not as much authority for considering God masculine, as we have for considering Him feminine, for Love imparts the clearest idea of Deity.” (Science and Health 517:8)
  • “Man and woman as coexistent and eternal with God forever reflect, in glorified quality, the infinite Father-Mother God.” (Science and Health 516:21)
  • “. . . bring my sons from far, and my daughters from the ends of the earth;” (Isaiah 43:6)

Illustration

We used the fish asking “What is water?” from David Foster Wallace's 2005 Kenyon College commencement speech, “This Is Water,” including this line: “…the most obvious, important realities are often the ones that are hardest to see and talk about.” We also used the light spectrum analogy to add color to the Bartimaeus story.

Case study outline: The note that cracked

Original, fictional junior-high case about an old label. Last May, Emma's trumpet solo cracked on the highest note at the spring concert, the audience laughed, and by Monday some kids in band were calling her “Crack.” By September the name had stuck, so she quit volunteering and moved to third chair on purpose. Then the band director offered her the winter concert solo and asked her to decide by Friday. Her friend Jake told her to go for it because nobody remembers. Emma answered that everybody remembers, and that she is just the girl who cracks. Ask the class: What is Emma actually afraid of, the note or the name?

We got close to completing the case in class, but we did not finish. Plan to continue next week. The take-home worksheet asks students to write out their own treatments, not just read, in four steps used as a way to practice what Mary Baker Eddy teaches, not as a formula she named: 1) Addressing the fear, take off the old clothes (Science and Health 411:27, Mark 10:50, Science and Health 262:7); 2) What we know about God (Science and Health 465:9, 502:29); 3) What we know about man (Jeremiah 1:5, II Corinthians 5:4); 4) Gratitude (Psalms 30:11, Science and Health 3:22).
